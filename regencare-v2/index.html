The patient’s dilemma

Most people are offered two choices. Live with it, or have surgery.

Painkillers manage a symptom. Surgery replaces a structure. Neither asks the tissue to repair itself. Regenerative medicine is the option in between, and for many of the conditions we treat it is the one that should come first.

The Regencare approach

Four visits, and nothing that is not already yours

Every treatment follows the same discipline: understand the tissue, take what the body already produces, concentrate it, and place it precisely.

01 Consultation and imaging
02 Drawing your own sample
03 Concentration in-house
04 Guided placement and recovery
01

We start with the tissue, not the prescription

A specialist examines the joint or the skin, reviews imaging, and establishes whether regenerative therapy is appropriate for the condition in front of them. If it is not, we say so.

02

A small draw of blood, marrow or fat

The source depends on the therapy: blood for PRP, GFC and conditioned serum, bone marrow for BMAC, adipose tissue for stromal vascular fraction. It is taken in the same visit.

03

Concentrated on site, within the hour

Platelets, growth factors or cells are separated and concentrated in our own lab. Nothing is shipped, nothing is stored, and nothing synthetic is added.

04

Placed precisely, then you walk out

The concentrate is injected into the joint, tendon or scalp under guidance. Most procedures are day-care, with comparatively quick recovery and lasting results.

Loss to follow-up and onward-surgery rate are what make the other numbers believable. A clinic that hides them is asking to be taken on faith.

Are you a candidate?

This will not tell you whether treatment works. It will tell you whether you are worth assessing.

Regenerative treatment suits some people and not others, and the honest answer often depends on an image we have not seen yet. Here is roughly where the line sits.

Usually worth assessing

  • Early to moderate joint degeneration, where cartilage and joint space remain
  • Pain that has persisted despite physiotherapy and medication
  • You have been offered surgery but want to know whether it can be delayed
  • Sports or tendon injury with a defined recovery deadline
  • Hair thinning that is still active rather than long-established
  • Acne scarring where the skin is otherwise healthy

Usually not, and we will say so

  • End-stage joint destruction with bone-on-bone contact and no joint space
  • Active infection in or near the target area
  • Active malignancy, or blood disorders affecting platelet function
  • An unstable joint that needs mechanical repair, not biological support
  • Complete baldness with no viable follicles remaining
  • Expectations of a permanent cure — this is management, often repeated
