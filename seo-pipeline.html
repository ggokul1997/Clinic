Inside the Marketing & SEO agent

"Marketing & SEO" is not one agent. It is a nineteen-component pipeline — but only six of those components are genuine agents. The rest is ordinary code, and saying so is the point of this version.

6true agents (need a model)
3hybrid (one model call + code)
10deterministic functions
2human sign-offs

What changed in v2, and why

This version was rewritten against a hostile review of v1. Four criticisms were accepted and fixed.

Criticism of v1Fix in v2
"Most of these aren't agents, they're functions with a costume on." Every component is now labelled Agent Hybrid or Code against a stated test. Six are agents. Ten are plain code.
"Your build order doesn't run." Correct — Phase 1 called for a brief generator whose input came from a component scheduled in Phase 5. Rebuilt so Phase 1 executes, with the gap filled by a human choosing the topic.
"Every headline statistic comes from a vendor selling the solution." The load-bearing figure now cites the underlying study with its methodology and confidence interval. Every other figure is labelled by source quality.
"Happy path only. No failure handling, no evaluation, no compliance." Three new sections, plus a nineteenth component: a compliance check.
The honest count is a stronger argument, not a weaker one.

Nineteen "agents" reads as inflation. Six agents and ten deterministic functions reads as engineering — and it is cheaper, faster and easier to test.

The test for what counts as an agent

A component is an agent only if it must make a judgement under uncertainty that we cannot write down as a rule. If the behaviour can be specified — a threshold, a template, a query, a validation — it is code, and should be built as code: cheaper, faster, deterministic, and testable.

Model choice for M07 and M08 — a research finding that reverses the obvious answer.

A study of 11 foundation models across 7 medical hallucination tasks found general-purpose models produced hallucination-free responses far more often than medical-specialised ones — median 76.6% versus 51.3% (difference 25.2%, 95% CI 18.7–31.3%, p = 0.012). The authors attribute this to safety emerging from broad reasoning ability rather than narrow domain tuning.

The same study found chain-of-thought prompting reduced hallucinations in 86.4% of tested comparisons. In a survey of 70 clinicians, 91.8% had encountered medical hallucinations and 84.7% judged them capable of causing patient harm.

Design consequence: do not reach for a medical-specialised model here. Use a strong general-purpose model, with explicit reasoning traces and retrieval grounding — and keep verification as a separate component, because the checker must not be the author.

Source: Medical Hallucination in Foundation Models and Their Impact on Healthcare, medRxiv 2025.02.28.25323115 / arXiv 2503.05777. Caveat: preprint, n = 11 models, n = 70 clinicians. Treat as strong evidence, not settled fact.

M18 is new, and it is deliberately dumb.

Medical advertising rules vary by jurisdiction and change. The component applies a rule list; it does not interpret law. Who owns and updates that rule list is an open question — see the final section. Until it has an owner, M18 is a placeholder, not a control.

The gate

Two signatures, and neither is an agent

Editorial sign-off A marketer checks tone, brand, positioning and search strategy.
Clinical sign-off A named clinician checks the medical claims and becomes the article's reviewedBy on the record.

Anything carrying untrusted-external provenance requires the clinical signature regardless of topic. The component that drafted a page is never one of its approvers. The draft-only restriction is declared once on the collection, so it applies to every route in — not just the button in the interface.

STAGE 6Monitoring— and back to the start

A refreshed medical page goes through the gate again.

Re-entering at Stage 2 means a refresh gets the same citation verification, the same compliance screen and the same clinical signature as a new article. Clinical guidance changes; approval from eighteen months ago is not approval today.

When it fails

v1 described only the happy path. These are the failure modes that actually occur, and what the pipeline does about each.

FailureBehaviourEscalation
A citation will not resolve Claim flagged, never silently dropped. Draft continues with the claim marked unverified. Clinician decides: cite differently, soften, or remove. Cannot pass M14 while flagged.
Three or more claims unverifiable Draft returned to M07 with the flagged claims. One retry only. Second failure retires the topic and notifies the editor. No infinite loops.
Clinician review not started in 14 days Draft moves to a stalled queue and stops consuming attention. Editor is notified at 7 days, clinical lead at 14. Queue depth is capped.
Review queue exceeds capacity M06 stops scheduling new drafts. The pipeline throttles itself rather than piling up. Backlog reported weekly. This is the real capacity control — see open questions.
Keyword API quota exhausted M01 serves cached data and marks results stale. Pipeline continues on stale data for up to 7 days, then pauses discovery.
A component errors repeatedly Three retries with backoff, then the job moves to a dead-letter queue. Dead-letter reviewed daily. The pipeline does not stop; other jobs proceed.
Compliance flag raised Hard stop. Cannot pass M14 under any circumstances. Routed to whoever owns the rule list. No agent may clear a compliance flag.

The data layer

Everything upstream depends on three APIs, all of which meter and throttle. v1 said "keyword APIs" and left it there.

SourceReal constraintConsequence for design
Google Search Console API Free, but capped around 50,000 page/keyword pairs per property per day, with QPS, QPM and QPD limits. Fine at our scale. Pull nightly, cache locally, never query live.
Semrush API Requires the top toolkit tier (~$549/mo); units bought separately at roughly $50 per million. 10 requests/sec, 10 concurrent. Expensive. Only justified once discovery is automated — not in Phase 1.
Ahrefs API Tiered units — Lite 10k, Standard 150k, Advanced 500k per month. 60 requests/min, max 25 rows per request, minimum 50 units per call. The 25-row cap forces pagination; budget units per run or M01 stalls mid-job.
PubMed / guideline bodies Free, rate-limited, no commercial licence needed for verification lookups. Cache aggressively — the same citations recur across articles.
Start on Search Console alone.

It is free and covers our own performance data, which is all Phases 1–4 need. Paid keyword APIs become worth their cost only when M01 and M03 run automatically at volume.

Compute time is irrelevant to throughput.

An article is machine-ready in under two hours and then waits days for a clinician. Optimising the agents does not increase output. Only reviewer capacity does — which is why the first open question below governs the whole design.

Risk: Google's scaled content abuse policy

This is a live risk for exactly the system we are describing.

Google's policy is method-neutral — it does not penalise content for being AI-generated. It penalises generating many pages primarily to manipulate rankings with little added value. Enforcement through 2026 has reportedly hit sites publishing large volumes of AI-generated pages without editorial oversight, and the spam policies now apply to what appears inside AI Overviews as well as ordinary results.

Our mitigation is structural rather than promissory: every page carries a named clinician reviewer, verified citations, and a human editorial signature. That is the difference between an editorial operation that uses AI and a content farm. It is also why the volume ceiling set by reviewer capacity is a feature — it makes unreviewed mass publication impossible by construction.

Source quality: industry case-study reporting, not primary research. Directionally reliable; the specific traffic-drop figures should not be quoted as fact.

Open questions — not answered here

Two numbers govern this design, and we do not have either

1. How many hours per week can clinicians give to review?

This sets the ceiling on everything. If two doctors can give two hours a week and a proper medical review takes 30–45 minutes, the system tops out near 5–6 articles a week no matter how good the agents are.

How to answer it: ask the clinical lead for a committed weekly figure, then time three real reviews to get the true per-article cost. Then resize this pipeline to that number. If the answer is 5 a week, several components in Phases 3–5 are not worth building.

2. What does one article cost to produce?

Unknown until measured. The variable is M08 — one retrieval and verification cycle per clinical claim, on a long context.

How to answer it: instrument token usage per component during Phase 1 and report cost per article alongside the Phase 2b baseline. The comparison that matters is against a freelance medical writer at market rate.

3. Who owns the compliance rule list?

M18 applies rules; it does not interpret law. Medical advertising rules vary by jurisdiction and change over time. Until a named person owns and maintains that list, M18 is a placeholder rather than a control — and this document should not be read as claiming compliance coverage.
